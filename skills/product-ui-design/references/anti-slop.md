# Product-specific visual direction

"AI slop" is not a specific color, font, radius, or framework. It is the visible result of making
styling decisions without product evidence: interchangeable hierarchy, unearned decoration,
fabricated content, and repeated components that ignore the task.

## Find a visual thesis

Describe in one sentence how this product should organize attention and why. Ground it in one or
more observed facts:

- the material users manipulate, such as prose, timelines, maps, code, media, or transactions
- whether the work is fast and repetitive, slow and considered, exploratory, or safety-critical
- the real content's density, variation, and failure modes
- an existing brand asset, physical analogy, editorial tradition, or domain convention

The thesis should resolve choices. “Quiet hierarchy for long review sessions” can justify restrained
contrast and dense evidence. “A living canvas for arranging sound” can justify spatial controls and
direct manipulation. “Modern, clean, premium” does not resolve anything.

Prefer one memorable, useful expression of the thesis over many decorative gestures. Distinctiveness
may come from typography, composition, data representation, motion behavior, illustration, or the
shape of a core interaction; it does not require ornament.

## Challenge template reflexes

These patterns are not forbidden. Require a product-specific reason before using them:

- a purple, blue, or aurora gradient as the default identity
- glass panels, blurred blobs, glows, grain, or floating geometric decorations
- a large marketing hero inside a task-oriented authenticated product
- bento grids or stat-card dashboards regardless of information relationships
- cards around every section when spacing or a divider already establishes grouping
- pill containers for ordinary labels, navigation, and actions
- uniformly large corner radii and soft shadows on every surface
- gradient text, excessive display type, or centered prose that weakens reading
- icon-heavy controls, mixed icon families, or emoji used as interface symbols
- scroll reveals and hover motion applied to every element
- generic “seamless”, “unlock”, “supercharge”, or “next-generation” copy

If removing an effect leaves the meaning and hierarchy unchanged, the effect is optional. Keep it
only when it strengthens the stated visual thesis without harming usability or performance.

## Avoid synthetic completeness

Design around the content that actually exists. Use representative extremes such as long names,
missing images, zero results, errors, and dense records. When data is unavailable, show clearly
marked placeholders or an honest empty state.

Never fabricate impressive numbers, live activity, customer logos, quotes, avatars, ratings, awards,
or integrations. Do not hide weak information architecture behind polished sample data.

## Review questions

- Could this screen belong to an unrelated product after changing the logo and accent color?
- Which decision came from user behavior, content, brand, or platform evidence?
- Is the primary task clearer because of each prominent visual treatment?
- Are component shapes reflecting semantics, or merely repeating one fashionable primitive?
- Does the interface still have hierarchy without gradients, shadows, and animation?
- Is any content pretending the product has evidence or capabilities it does not have?
- Is the design recognizably one system without making every region look the same?

If the first answer is yes and the remaining answers are weak, revise the design basis before adding
more polish.
